Editorial Standards

How OmniTrackr writes, checks and corrects its guides, puzzles and public pages, and the rules member reviews follow before anyone else can read them.

Who writes the guides

OmniTrackr is an independent site run by one person. The In order, If you liked and Discover guides, the monthly editions, the best-of lists, the release guides, the seasonal pages and the Daily puzzles are written for OmniTrackr. Drafts are written with the help of AI writing tools. reads, edits and fact-checks every guide before it is published, and again whenever it is updated, and decides what goes in and what is cut.

The guides do not claim first-hand experience. When a guide says a book is slow to start or a game asks for patience, that is an editorial reading of the work and of how it is widely described, not a report of someone's weekend. We would rather say that plainly than invent a reviewer.

No studio, publisher, platform or advertiser pays to appear in a guide, to be recommended or to be placed higher in one. Advertising never decides what a guide covers.

How facts are checked

Release years, creators, cast, episode and book counts, and the order in which stories take place are checked against public sources, preferring the official page of the studio, publisher, broadcaster or author. Discover guides list every source they use, with the date it was checked. Where sources disagree, the guide follows the official one and says that editions differ.

Release years are for the first release in the original language. Running times, page counts, editions and where a title can be streamed or bought vary by region and over time, so guides describe them loosely ("around ten hours", "a long novel"). A precise number might not hold where you are.

Facts and opinions are kept apart. Comparisons of mood, pace, difficulty and who a title suits are labelled as editorial opinion. A recommended order through a franchise is the order we would hand a friend starting from nothing; the guide always gives the alternatives and says who they suit better.

How picks are chosen

  • A reason for every pick. Each title is in a guide because of something specific it shares with the starting point, and the guide says what that is.
  • A caveat for every pick. Each one also says who it may not suit, or what is different, so you can skip it with a clear conscience.
  • More than one format. "If you liked" and Discover guides mix films, series, anime, games, books and albums, because the feeling you are chasing is rarely tied to one format.
  • No popularity rankings. Guides are not ranked by charts or scores. The best-of lists and release guides start from what came out or is coming out, but what gets in is our call, and a small, odd pick that fits beats a famous one that does not.

How each kind of guide is made

The guides share the rules on this page, but each kind starts from a different question, so each is put together a little differently.

In order

An In order guide answers "what order do I watch, read or play this in?" for one franchise. We list the entries most people mean by the franchise's name, check each release year and where it sits in the story, and mark side stories as optional when the main story makes sense without them. Every guide gives two to four complete orders and says who each one suits, and the first one listed is the order we recommend. Where an order would give away a twist that release order protects, the guide says so before you start.

If you liked

An If you liked guide starts by naming the three things its title does well. Every pick has to match at least one of them, and the guide says which. Seven or eight picks are spread across at least three formats, because the feeling you want after a favourite is rarely tied to one format. Each pick has a short pitch, a comparison with the original, and a caveat.

Discover guides and monthly editions

A Discover trail connects four titles in different formats through one idea, such as found family or clever mysteries, and compares them by pace and commitment so you can choose by the evening you have. Every Discover guide lists its sources and what each source was used to confirm. The monthly editions are shorter: six picks around a theme for the month, with an essay explaining the theme.

Best of the year

The best-of lists pick eight or nine releases from the year in one format. We start from the releases that critics and audiences kept returning to, then keep the ones we would recommend to a friend, with a range of genres. Each list names its sources, and scores and dates are checked against them.

Release guides

The release guides cover what is coming out in a month or a season. Most of the titles are not out yet when the guide is written, so they are previews, not reviews. A title needs an announced date or month to be included, and each one says who should wait for reviews. Dates move, so these guides are rechecked more often than the others, and when a date is still only a month the guide says so.

Seasonal guides and the gift guide

The Halloween and winter holiday pages have four shelves of six picks, one for each kind of night or break people plan around, such as a film night with friends or something the whole family can watch. Every pick is placed on a five-step scale, from "spooky, not scary" to "lights on afterwards" in October and from "just wintry" to "full tinsel" in December, so you can choose how far to go. The gift guide gives four gifts for each of nine kinds of fan, at different sizes. The Black Friday guide goes format by format through what tends to get cheaper that week and what rarely does, with three things to watch for in each and no prices, because they change by the hour. These two are the guides whose shop links are affiliate links, and each page says so before the first link. Beside the holiday page, the Thanksgiving weekend guide and the December reading and listening guide follow one part of the season in order, with five picks for each day or kind of time and no festive scale.

Switching guides

The switching guides explain how to export a library from another service and bring it into OmniTrackr. Each one is checked against OmniTrackr's Import Studio, and says which fields come across and which don't.

Spoilers

Guides describe premises, tone and structure, not endings. Mysteries keep their solutions, and franchise guides warn you before an order would give away a twist that the release order protects. The answer to a Daily puzzle stays hidden until you choose to reveal it, and today's answer never appears anywhere on the site until you have finished playing.

Corrections and updates

Every guide shows when it was first published and when it was last checked. If you find a wrong date, a missing entry, an outdated link or a spoiler, send a correction. Corrections are made on the page itself, the checked date moves forward, and a significant change is noted in What's new. If a guide's recommendation changes, the page says so.

Guides are also reread when a franchise gets a new entry or a source page changes. If we can't keep a guide accurate any more, we take it down.

Member reviews and collections

Everything in a member's library is private unless they choose otherwise. A review is public only when its author ticks Public Review on that one entry, and a collection is public only when its owner publishes it. Ratings, progress, private notes and the rest of the library are never shown alongside it.

Public reviews have to say something. A review appears in public lists once it has at least 80 characters of varied writing, and gets a page of its own only at 240 characters and 35 words, written as complete thoughts. Reviews that contain links, email addresses, phone numbers or promotional wording are kept out of public pages altogether; they stay in the author's own library, untouched. The review guidelines explain what makes a review worth reading for someone else.

Anyone can report a public review or collection by choosing a fixed reason. After reports from three separate browsers, that exact version is hidden from public pages and its author is told. Nothing is deleted: editing the review or collection substantively puts it back if it still meets the rules.

What we do not publish

  • Plot summaries, reviews or descriptions copied from other sites or databases.
  • Lists of titles with nothing to say about them.
  • Pages written to catch a search term that don't answer the question behind it.
  • Claims about features OmniTrackr does not have, or comparisons that leave out where another service is the better choice.
  • Pages where advertising is more prominent than what you came to read.

Some title pages show artwork and basic details supplied by public databases such as TVmaze, Open Library and Wikipedia, credited on the page. Those details help you recognise a title, but they are not OmniTrackr's writing, so a title page is only listed for search once a member has written a substantial public review of it.
