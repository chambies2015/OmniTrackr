Why track music in a media library?

Music listening is often scattered across streaming services, playlists, recommendations, purchases, and memory. A streaming app can show recent listening, but it won't keep why an album meant something to you, what you thought after repeat listens, or how your music taste connects to movies, games, books, and shows.

In OmniTrackr, music is one more category in your media collection. You can track title, artist, year, genre, listened status, rating, review, and cover art. So you can find the album you played all through one summer, the record a friend told you to try, or the one you meant to go back to.

With music next to your other media, you start to see connections. A favorite album might share a mood with a favorite film, a game soundtrack, or a book you read during the same month. You can note that in OmniTrackr instead of keeping a separate journal.

A light music tracking routine

  1. Add albums or releases when they are recommended, saved, or listened to.
  2. Record artist, year, and genre so the list remains searchable later.
  3. Use listened status to separate albums you've heard from the ones you haven't got to yet.
  4. Rate after a few listens, once the album is no longer new to you.
  5. Write review notes about mood, production, lyrics, replay value, favorite moments, or context.

Music reviews don't need to read like formal criticism. A few sentences about when an album works best are often enough to help you remember it and recommend it.

Albums, artists, and relisten notes

OmniTrackr tracks music at the album level, since an album is long enough to rate, review, and come back to. You can add studio albums, EPs, live records, soundtracks, compilations, or any other release you want in your history.

Relisten notes help when your opinion changes. Some albums click immediately; others need the right mood, speakers, season, or context. A short review can record whether the album improved after repeat listens, whether a standout track carried the score, or whether the full release is stronger than its singles.

  • Use artist and year fields to find older favorites quickly.
  • Use genres loosely when a record crosses styles or moods.
  • Use ratings after a few listens when first-impression excitement has settled.
  • Use reviews to remember favorite tracks, production details, lyrics, pacing, and replay value.

Writing music reviews you'll want to reread

Music notes are mostly about how a record feels. Mention whether an album is energetic, quiet, dense, immediate, nostalgic, difficult, background-friendly, or built for focused listening. If your opinion changed after several listens, write that down.

Public reviews need a little context. A line like "best for late-night listening" or "great production but uneven pacing" tells other listeners more than a score does.

Private music notes can stay messy and personal. Public music reviews deserve a bit more care: say the type of listener who might enjoy the release, whether the album works front-to-back, and what someone should know before pressing play.

Music statistics and taste patterns

Music statistics can reveal whether your library leans toward certain genres, years, or artists. When music sits beside your other media, you can also see broader taste patterns: maybe your favorite games and favorite albums share a mood, or your highest-rated books and records cluster around the same period.

Statistics also help you tidy up. If you have many saved albums with no rating, that may be a relisten queue. If your highest-rated albums have no reviews, start writing notes there. If one artist takes up a big share of the library, try similar artists, genres, or eras next.

Privacy, sharing, and recommendations

Music taste can feel personal, so OmniTrackr lets you decide what stays private and which reviews become public. You can keep a messy listening log for yourself, share some impressions with friends, or publish a finished review for other listeners.

So the same library works as a private listening diary and as a way to pass on recommendations. A friend doesn't need your whole library to know why one album is worth hearing. One clear public review or shared note does the job.
