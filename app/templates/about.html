About OmniTrackr

A free, independent tracker for everything you watch, play, read and hear, and a library of free guides to what to try next.

What OmniTrackr is

OmniTrackr keeps movies, TV shows, anime, video games, albums, books, manga, podcasts and audiobooks in one private library. You can rate them, write notes and reviews, save the episode or page you stopped at, and see how your taste changes over time. It runs in the browser on any device, it is free, and you can export everything whenever you like.

It exists because media habits rarely stay inside one app. A streaming service remembers its own catalog, a storefront remembers purchases, and a notes app holds loose recommendations from friends. None of them gives you the whole picture: what you meant to try, what you finished, what you loved, what you dropped and what you would hand to a friend. OmniTrackr is built around that whole picture.

What you can read without an account

Most of the public site is written for people who will never sign up, and that is fine. Everything below is free to read, and the guide library lists every guide by format and by kind:

  • In order guides walk through long franchises such as Star Wars, The Witcher, Dune and Zelda. Each gives more than one order, says who each one suits and recommends one.
  • If you liked guides start from one favourite and suggest what to try next in other formats, with a caveat for every pick.
  • Discover guides connect four titles in different formats through one idea, such as a film, an anime, a game and a book about finding your feet. They compare the four by pace and commitment and link the sources used to check the facts. A monthly edition on the Discover page adds a new theme each month.
  • OmniTrackr Daily is a guess-the-title puzzle with a new movie, show, anime, game, book or album every day. Past puzzles stay playable, with the answer and a short note on why the title is worth your time.
  • The Essential 100 is nine lists of 100 all-time titles each, from films and albums to manga, podcasts and audiobooks, with why each one is there and where to start. Every list doubles as a checklist you can tick off.
  • Best of the year lists pick the year's best new releases in nine formats, from movies and anime to manga, podcasts and audiobooks. Each pick says why it made the cut and what might put you off.
  • Seasonal guides follow the calendar. The Halloween and winter holiday pages sort their picks by how spooky or how festive you want the evening to be. Beside them are a Thanksgiving weekend guide, a Christmas 2026 guide with a dated calendar of the year's new Christmas releases, a December reading and listening guide, a gift guide, a Black Friday guide and a Valentine's Day guide for February 2027. From December 1 an advent calendar opens one door a day until Christmas Eve.
  • Awards season pages cover the Game Awards, the Golden Globes, the Oscars and the Grammys: when each one is, how the winners are chosen and how to watch, with a ballot for your own picks once the official nominations are out.
  • The Binge calculator works out how long a show, anime, book series or film series takes and the day you'd finish at your pace. Thirty long series, from Breaking Bad to One Piece and The Wheel of Time, have their own guide with season-by-season numbers and what the total leaves out.
  • Release guides pick the month's and the season's releases worth planning around, including a guide to each new anime season, and say plainly who should wait for the reviews.
  • Browse shelves hand-pick manga, podcasts and audiobooks to start with, with a line on who each suits and, for audiobooks, who reads it and how long it runs.
  • The tier list maker and the group poll are free tools for ranking what you've seen and for picking what to watch with friends. Neither needs an account to use. The break planner, Watch together and Pick swap need a free account, because they work from your own library or with your friends, but each page explains how it works first, and the break planner has a sample plan anyone can try.
  • Monthly challenges set one small goal each month, such as finishing something from before 2000, with suggestions for every format.
  • Member pages come from what members choose to share. A title page collects public reviews of that title once enough has been written about it, director and author pages rank their work by member ratings, and title discussions are open to read. Pages with only a line or two of member writing stay out of search until there is more to read.
  • Release Radar lists what comes out this month, and the how-to guides explain tracking, importing from other services and backing up a library. If you are choosing a tracker, our comparison sets OmniTrackr next to IMDb, Letterboxd, Trakt and Goodreads and says where each one is the better fit.

Who runs it

OmniTrackr is an independent project run by one person. It is not part of a studio, publisher, streaming service or media company, and nobody pays to be included in a guide or to be ranked higher in one. The code that runs the site is public on GitHub, so anyone can read how the tracker works and how private data is handled.

Every guide is reviewed by the site owner before it is published. Drafts are written with AI writing tools. Release years, cast, creators and story order are then checked against public and official sources, and the Discover guides link every source they use. Opinions about mood, pace and who a title suits are labelled as editorial opinion, never presented as facts or as first-hand experience. Every guide shows the date it was last checked. The editorial standards page explains the rules in full.

If something is wrong, out of date or spoils more than it should, tell us. Corrections are made on the page itself, the checked date moves forward, and the change goes in the corrections log, which lists what each guide used to say.

How it got here

Work on OmniTrackr began in September 2025. By the end of that November it tracked movies, TV shows and anime, video games followed in December, and music and books in February 2026.

The written guides started in September 2026 with ten Discover guides. The If you liked and In order guides followed in October, the same month manga, podcasts and audiobooks got shelves of their own and the tracker reached nine formats. November added the "how long does it take" guides, the best of the year in all nine formats, awards ballots, the holiday guides and the Essential 100. It also brought the first guides for 2027, to the winter and spring releases and to Valentine's Day, and a public log of every correction made to a guide.

There are written guides today. Each shows the date it was first published and the date it was last checked, and What's new lists the tracker's releases since June 2026.

How it is paid for

OmniTrackr has no paywall: every tracking feature is free and stays free. The site owner pays for hosting. Three things help cover it: advertising on some public guide pages, Amazon affiliate links on a few pages that say so, and readers who choose to support the site on Ko-fi, either once or with a monthly membership from $3. Memberships add cosmetic extras such as profile banners and a supporter badge; they never unlock tracking features. Signed-in members do not see ads, and ads never appear inside a private library. The ads and affiliates page lists where ads can appear and how affiliate links are labelled, and the supporters page thanks the people who chip in.

Your library stays yours

Libraries are private by default. A review becomes public only when you tick Public Review on that one entry, and you choose separately what friends can see in each category. Your personal information is not sold, and nothing in your library is shared with advertisers; the privacy policy has the detail.

You can export your library and journal as a file at any time and import it again later, so years of history are never trapped here. If you are coming from another service, the switching guide shows how to bring your lists with you.

How it changes

OmniTrackr is updated most weeks. What's new lists every release, and the roadmap says what is being considered next. Updates are additive: a new feature never removes or rewrites what is already in your library.
