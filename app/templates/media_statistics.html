What statistics add to a media tracker

Statistics answer questions your memory can't. What category do you finish most often? Which media type gets your highest ratings? Are you adding more to the backlog than you complete? Which years, directors, genres, or formats dominate your taste history?

OmniTrackr statistics look across everything you've saved. Along with your list, the dashboard summarizes completion, rating coverage, review coverage, category size, top-rated items, year trends, and category-specific details.

Without them, a media library can turn into a pile of titles. Statistics show whether you finish what you add, whether your ratings are consistent enough to compare, and where you've written a lot of reviews or almost none.

Numbers worth watching

  • Completion percentage: shows how much of your library is finished versus waiting.
  • Rating coverage: shows whether ratings are consistent enough to compare categories fairly.
  • Review coverage: shows how much context you are preserving beyond a score.
  • Top category: shows where most of your tracking activity is happening.
  • Most complete category: shows which media type you are actually closing out.
  • Public reviews: shows how much of your writing you've chosen to share.
  • Time spent: estimates the hours behind everything you've finished or are partway through, using running times, episode lengths, page counts and typical game lengths. The section says how many titles used a typical length because nothing more exact was known. To plan the other way round, the binge calculator works out how long a series will take at your pace.

These numbers say something about what you do, where a raw count only says how much you've added. A huge backlog can look impressive, but completion and review coverage tell you whether the library is helping you choose well.

Category-specific insights

For movies, look at directors and decades. For TV shows and anime, look at seasons and episodes. For video games, look at genres and played status. Music and books sit on the same statistics dashboard, so all your ratings and reviews are in one place instead of spread across different apps.

  • Movies: spot favorite directors, year ranges, average ratings, watched status, and which films deserve a better review.
  • TV and anime: compare seasons, episodes, completed series, stalled shows, and longer viewing commitments.
  • Games: review genre patterns, played status, backlog weight, replay favorites, and whether highly rated games are actually finished.
  • Music: see albums and artists next to your other categories, so your listening history counts alongside everything else.
  • Books: track reading progress, authors, review coverage, and how your reading compares with films, shows, games, and music.
  • Manga: compare volumes and chapters read across series, and see which ones you stopped partway through.
  • Podcasts: see which shows you keep up with, which limited series you finished, and how many episodes are waiting.
  • Audiobooks: count the hours in the audiobooks you finished and on your whole shelf, and see which genres you listen to most.

Using review coverage

Review coverage is worth checking often because it shows how many entries have more than a score. A high rating is a start, but a short note explaining why something worked is easier to trust a year later. If your top-rated items have no reviews, that is a good place to start writing.

Public review counts are separate from private notes. You can keep private reviews as rough reminders and publish only the ones you've written up for other people. The dashboard counts both, and nothing pushes you to make a note public.

How to use the numbers

  1. Clean up categories with low completion and high backlog counts.
  2. Add reviews to highly rated items so you remember why they worked.
  3. Compare ratings across categories before making recommendations.
  4. Use year and genre patterns to choose something outside your usual habits.
  5. Export now and then so you have your own copy of the data behind the statistics.

A monthly cleanup can be short: check your biggest backlog category, mark anything you no longer care about, add reviews to your five highest-rated items without notes, and export a backup after the cleanup. That way you act on the statistics instead of just glancing at them.

Reading statistics without overthinking them

Treat a statistic as the start of a question, and don't read it as the answer. A low average rating in one category might mean you are choosing poorly, but it might also mean you are more critical of that format. A high backlog count might mean excitement, or it might mean a category needs cleanup.

Let the numbers nudge what you do next. If a genre dominates your unfinished games, pause before buying another similar title. If your favorite movies have no reviews, write the context while you still remember it. If books have lower completion but higher ratings, that may just mean books take longer, and you're no less interested.

Because OmniTrackr keeps each category in the same account, you can compare them directly, as long as you remember that a book and a movie take very different amounts of time.

Privacy and sharing

Statistics belong to your account. You can keep categories and statistics private, share some of them with friends where privacy settings allow it, and choose which reviews are public. Seeing your own patterns never requires putting your library on public view.

If you're new, this is worth knowing up front: you can learn about your own habits in OmniTrackr and still decide what stays private, what friends can see, and which reviews you're ready to publish.
